# Simplified Technical English — condensed rule guide

A working summary of the ASD-STE100 Issue 9 writing rules, for writers and for
tools that check STE. It states every rule and the guidance needed to apply it.
It is a summary, not the standard: it carries no examples, no explanatory text,
and no dictionary entries. Get the standard itself, free, from
<https://www.asd-ste100.org>.

ASD-STE100 Simplified Technical English is the property of ASD and its name is
an EU registered trade mark. Rule numbers here match Issue 9 (2025-01-15) so any
finding can be looked up in the published document.

## What STE is

A controlled natural language for technical documentation. Two parts:

- **Writing rules** (part 1): 9 sections, 53 rules, plus 8 general
  recommendations. Grammar and style.
- **Dictionary** (part 2): about 875 approved words, each with one part of
  speech and usually one meaning, plus about 1274 words that are not approved,
  each with approved alternatives.

It exists because English is the working language of aerospace and defense
documentation but usually not the reader's first language. English words carry
many meanings and many synonyms, and a misread maintenance instruction can kill
someone. STE removes the ambiguity.

STE is not used alone. It works with the applicable specification for technical
publications, style guide, and official directives for the project. It does not
regulate abbreviations, units of measurement, or formatting.

## The vocabulary

**Case is the convention.** In the dictionary a word in UPPERCASE is approved. A
word in lowercase is not approved, and its entry gives approved alternatives
instead of a meaning.

**Parts of speech.** Eight: noun (n), verb (v), adjective (adj), adverb (adv),
pronoun (pron), article (art), preposition (prep), conjunction (conj). An
approved word is approved *as one part of speech only*. A few are approved as
more than one — CLEAN is both a verb and an adjective.

**Technical nouns and technical verbs** are the escape hatch that makes the
vocabulary usable. A technical noun is a noun term for a specified concept in a
subject field; a technical verb is the same for a process. They are not in the
dictionary, and rules 1.5 and 1.12 permit them. The dictionary marks them (TN)
and (TV) when it offers one as an alternative. So the dictionary is a core
vocabulary, not the whole permitted vocabulary — a word's absence from it is not
an error.

**Forms.** Nouns are listed in the singular; the plural of a countable noun is
permitted. Verbs are listed with every permitted form (base, present, simple
past, past participle) and no other form may be used. Adjectives are listed in
the base form with comparative and superlative in parentheses; where those are
made with "more" and "most" they are not listed, because MORE and MOST are
themselves approved. Adverbs are usually made by adding "-ly" to an adjective.

**Help.** Some entries carry a light-bulb symbol with a note, in four
categories: (1) how to use an approved word correctly, (2) an approved word has
a restricted meaning, so use the alternatives for other meanings, (3) a word
approved for one context only — SWALLOW is for safety instructions only, and
(4) important information about the listed word.

**Meanings.** An approved word may be used only with the meaning the dictionary
gives. If a meaning is not listed, the word may not be used for it. Approved
meanings and spelling follow American English and Merriam-Webster.

### Most frequent errors

The published list of recurring errors, condensed. Left is not approved, right
is what to use.

acceptable → PERMITTED (adj) · alternate → ALTERNATIVE (adj) · any → omit it or
use a different construction · avoid → PREVENT (v) · both → THE TWO (TN) ·
check (v) → CHECK (n) · cover (v) → COVER (TN) · complete (adj) → COMPLETED
(adj) · damage (v) → DAMAGE (n) · ensure → MAKE SURE (v) · fit → INSTALL (v) ·
follow → OBEY (v) · further → MORE · have to → an action verb in the command
form · however → BUT (conj) · insert → PUT (v) · main → PRIMARY (adj) · may →
CAN (v) · need → NECESSARY (adj) · now → AT THIS TIME · old → REMAINING, USED,
or EXPIRED (adj) · over → ABOVE, ON, or ALONG (prep) · people → PERSON or
PERSONNEL (n) · perform → DO (v) · portion → PART (n) · press → PUSH (v) ·
reach → GET (v) · repeat → DO (v) … AGAIN · required → NECESSARY (adj) ·
rotate → TURN (v) · secure → ATTACH (v) or SAFETY (v) · shall → MUST (v) ·
should → MUST (v) · since (conj) → BECAUSE (conj) · test (v) → TEST (n) ·
therefore → THUS (adv) or AS A RESULT · under → BELOW or IN (prep), or LESS
THAN · using → USE (v) or WITH (prep)

Note the pattern in `check`, `cover`, `damage`, `test`: the same spelling is
approved as a noun and rejected as a verb. These are part-of-speech questions
(rule 1.2), not forbidden words.

## Section 1 — Words

**1.1** Use words that are approved in the dictionary, technical nouns, or
technical verbs. Keep your technical nouns and verbs in a company glossary or
terminology database.

**1.2** Use an approved word only as the part of speech the dictionary
specifies. TEST is an approved noun but not an approved verb; DIM is an approved
adjective but not an approved verb. Where a word is approved as more than one
part of speech, its position in the sentence shows which is meant.

**1.3** Use an approved word only with its approved meaning. FALL means to move
down by the force of gravity, not to decrease.

**1.4** Use only the approved forms of verbs and adjectives.

**1.5** You may use a word that is not approved if it belongs to one of the 22
technical-noun categories. The categories cover: parts and equipment; materials
and substances; tools; places and locations; systems; measurable properties;
conditions and states; documents and data; personnel and organizations; times
and events; processes; environment; forces and energy; damage and defects;
quantities; software and computing; colors; shapes; law and regulations; and
animals, plants, and other life forms.

**1.6** Use a word that is not approved only when it is a technical noun or part
of one.

**1.7** Do not use a technical noun as a verb.

**1.8** Use technical nouns that are approved in your company, industry, or
subject field.

**1.9** When you must choose a technical noun, choose one that is short and easy
to understand.

**1.10** Do not use regional, slang, or jargon words as technical nouns.

**1.11** Do not use different technical nouns for the same item. One item, one
name, everywhere.

**1.12** You may use a verb that is not approved if it belongs to one of the
technical-verb categories: processes in a subject field, manufacturing
processes, computer processes and applications, and processes named after the
tool or material used.

**1.13** Do not use a technical verb as a noun.

**1.14** Use American English spelling unless an official directive says
otherwise.

## Section 2 — Multi-word nouns

A multi-word noun is a group of nouns and adjectives acting as one part of
speech, usually the subject or object. The head noun is normally the last word.
Long ones are ambiguous because the reader cannot tell how the words connect,
and in many languages the head noun comes first.

**2.1** Write multi-word nouns of no more than three words. "Runway light
connection" is fine; "runway light connection resistance calibration" is not.

**2.2** When a technical noun needs more than three words, write it in full the
first time. Then either give a shorter form of it, or use hyphens between the
words that act as one unit.

## Section 3 — Verbs

**3.1** Use only the verb forms given in the dictionary.

**3.2** Use only these forms and tenses: the infinitive; the imperative
(command) form; the simple present; the simple past; the simple future; and the
past participle as an adjective. Nothing else — no progressive, no perfect, no
conditional.

**3.3** Use the past participle form as an adjective, not to build other tenses.

**3.4** Do not use auxiliary verbs to make complex verb constructions.

**3.5** Use the "-ing" form of a verb only as a technical noun, or as a modifier
inside a technical noun. "Landing gear" is correct; "is operating" is not.

**3.6** Use the active voice. In descriptive writing the passive voice is
permitted only when the agent is unknown. Procedures use the command form and
therefore never the passive.

**3.7** Use an approved verb to describe an action, not a noun or another part
of speech. Write "the hydraulic fluid flow opens the valve", not "acts on the
valve".

## Section 4 — Sentences

The rules for sentences in procedures (section 5) differ from those for
descriptive text (section 6). Section 4 is what applies to both.

**4.1** Write short, clear sentences. One topic per sentence. In procedures give
the instruction directly to the reader in the command form. In descriptive text
give one subject or idea per sentence, then build on it.

**4.2** Do not omit words and do not use contractions to shorten a sentence.
Write "do not", never "don't".

**4.3** Use a vertical list for complex text. A list item may be a full
sentence or a fragment completing the lead-in. The colon at the end of the
lead-in ends a sentence for word-count purposes (rule 8.4).

**4.4** Use connecting words and phrases — BUT, THUS, AS A RESULT, THEN — to
connect sentences that hold related topics.

**4.5** Where applicable, put an article (the, a, an) or a demonstrative
adjective (this, these) before a noun or multi-word noun. Do not drop them to
save words.

## Section 5 — Procedural writing

**5.1** Write short sentences: **20 words maximum** per sentence. Warnings,
cautions, and other safety instructions must also obey this limit.

**5.2** Write one instruction per sentence, unless two or more actions happen at
the same time.

**5.3** Write instructions in the imperative (command) form. Start with the
verb.

**5.4** When the reader must know a condition first, start the instruction with
a descriptive statement, then separate it from the command with a comma: "If the
values are incorrect, stop the test procedure."

**5.5** Write notes to give information, not instructions. A note must not
contain a command.

## Section 6 — Descriptive writing

Descriptive writing gives information, not instructions, so the imperative form
is not permitted. It covers descriptions of items, products, systems and
components; general information such as reports and papers; and notes inside
procedures.

**6.1** Give information gradually. One subject per sentence, built up in order.

**6.2** Use key words and key phrases to give the text a logical structure.

**6.3** Write short sentences: **25 words maximum** per sentence.

**6.4** Use paragraphs to group related information.

**6.5** Give each paragraph one topic.

**6.6** No paragraph has more than **six sentences**.

## Section 7 — Safety instructions

A safety instruction warns that a procedure or work step can be dangerous or
cause damage. A **warning** tells the reader there is a risk of injury or death.
A **caution** tells the reader there is a risk of damage to objects. Other
industries may use different words — "danger", "attention", "notice" — or
graphical symbols; whatever words are used, the content must obey rules 7.1
through 7.3. Refer to ISO 45001, the ANSI Z535 series, and ISO 3864.

**7.1** Use an applicable word, for example "warning" or "caution", to identify
the level of risk.

**7.2** Start a safety instruction with a clear, accurate command or condition.

**7.3** Give an explanation that shows the risk or the possible result.

## Section 8 — Punctuation and word count

STE does not give general punctuation rules; follow a standard reference such as
the Chicago Manual of Style or the Gregg Reference Manual.

**8.1** All standard English punctuation is permitted **except the semicolon
(;)**. Write two sentences instead.

**8.2** Use hyphens (-) to connect words that are directly related.

**8.3** Parentheses are permitted to: make references to illustrations or text;
enclose letters or numbers identifying items on an illustration or in text;
identify work steps in a procedure; enclose abbreviations; give the singular and
plural of a noun at once; explain a word or part of a sentence; and give an
alternative.

**8.4** In a vertical list, a colon (:) counts the same as a period and ends a
sentence.

**8.5** Text in parentheses counts as **one word** in its sentence.

**8.6** Each of these counts as **one word**: a number; a number together with
its unit of measurement; an abbreviation; an alphanumeric identifier; quoted
text; a title, heading, placard, or label; and a proper noun of an individual,
group, organization, or geopolitical entity.

**8.7** A hyphenated word counts as **one word**.

### How to count a sentence

Apply 8.4 through 8.7 in this order:

1. Replace each parenthetical span with one word (8.5).
2. Replace each quoted span with one word (8.6).
3. Join each number to its following unit as one word (8.6).
4. Treat each abbreviation, alphanumeric identifier, and hyphenated word as one
   word (8.6, 8.7).
5. Count what remains. A colon ends the sentence (8.4).

Worked example: *"Install the three auxiliary screws (2) in the flange of the
motor assembly (9)."* — 14 words. Each parenthetical counts as one.

## Section 9 — Writing practices

**9.1** When a word-for-word replacement is not enough, use a different sentence
construction. If the dictionary's alternative has the same part of speech, swap
it in; if it has a different part of speech, rebuild the sentence. "A value of
2 mm is acceptable" becomes "A value of 2 mm is permitted" (same part of
speech); "Make sure that the valve is operable" becomes "Make sure that the
valve can operate" (different part of speech, so a new construction).

**9.2** Use each approved word correctly, with its approved part of speech and
meaning.

**9.3** When you use two words together, do not make phrasal verbs. A phrasal
verb means something different from its parts. Only a few — PUT ON, COME ON —
are approved, each with a restricted meaning.

**9.4** Use a consistent style. Once you choose terminology or wording for a
work step, use the same terminology and wording every time that step occurs.

## General recommendations

These are recommendations, not numbered rules.

**GR-1 — the conjunction "that".** Use it as much as possible after verbs such
as MAKE SURE, SHOW, and RECOMMEND. It marks where the main clause ends and the
subordinate clause begins, and many languages cannot omit the equivalent word.
Write "Make sure that the valve is open", not "Make sure the valve is open".

**GR-2 — the preposition "with".** It has three approved meanings: association
or relationship, help or sharing, and means or instrument. "Install the panel
with the green fasteners" has three possible readings. Reread every sentence
containing "with" and confirm it cannot be misread. When you rebuild such a
sentence, keep the primary action verb: write "Seal the opening with tool
TS9867", not "Use tool TS9867 to seal the opening".

**GR-3 — pronouns.** Use only the pronouns in the dictionary; "he" and "she" are
not approved. If a pronoun could refer to more than one noun, replace it with
the noun it refers to.

**GR-4 — the pronoun "this".** Make sure the reader knows what "this" refers to.
If it could refer to more than one item, restate the context.

**GR-5 — false friends.** A word that looks like one in the reader's native
language but means something different — English "disposition" against Italian
"disposizione". Confirm the English meaning is the one you intend.

**GR-6 — Latin abbreviations.** Do not use them. Write "for example" not "e.g.",
"that is" not "i.e.", and name the remaining items or write "and other colors"
instead of "etc." Often the abbreviation can simply be dropped.

**GR-7 — inclusive language.** Use neutral, non-discriminatory wording.
Gender-specific pronouns are not permitted. "Man" and "woman" are not permitted
unless the context requires them, for example in a medical text.

**GR-8 — possessive form.** The Saxon genitive ("the manufacturer's
instructions") is permitted but easy to misuse, and many languages form it
differently. If you are not sure the sentence is correct, do not use it.

## What a checker can decide, and what it cannot

Most of these rules need a word's part of speech **in context**. That needs a
parser. A dictionary-and-text checker can decide the rest.

Decidable from the text and the dictionary alone:

| Rule | Check |
| --- | --- |
| 1.1 | a word the dictionary lists as not approved |
| 1.5 | a word the dictionary offers elsewhere as a technical noun or verb (`FAILURE (TN)`) |
| 1.5 | a word rejected only as a verb but used here as a noun |
| 2.1 | a possible multi-word noun of more than three words |
| 3.2 | the "-ing" form used as a verb |
| 3.6 | passive voice |
| 4.2 | contractions |
| 5.1, 6.3 | sentence over 20 words (procedural) or 25 (descriptive) |
| 5.3 | an instruction that does not start with an approved verb |
| 6.6 | a paragraph of more than six sentences |
| 8.1 | the semicolon |
| GR-6 | Latin abbreviations |
| GR-8 | the possessive form |

Needs human judgement: part of speech in context (1.2 — a spelling approved
under any part of speech is accepted, because which one a sentence means cannot
be decided without a parser); approved meanings (1.3); technical noun selection
and consistency (1.8 through 1.11); shortening long technical nouns (2.2); text
structure and logic (6.1, 6.2, 6.4, 6.5); safety instruction content (7.1
through 7.3); hyphen and parenthesis usage (8.2, 8.3); phrasal verbs (9.3);
consistent style (9.4); and GR-1 through GR-5, GR-7.

Two cautions for anyone acting on checker output:

- A word missing from the dictionary is **not** an error. Rules 1.5 and 1.12
  permit any technical noun or verb from the subject field, and those are absent
  from the dictionary by definition.
- A word the dictionary rejects as a verb is often correct as a noun. `fuel (v)`
  is not approved, but "drain the fuel from the tank" is good STE, because FUEL
  there is a technical noun. Findings of this kind are prompts to check, not
  defects.
